Results: Gait vs PRS

The scatterplot showed a weak negative trend between PRS and mean absolute gait imbalance (see first scatterplot in previous section).

Normality was assessed using QQ plots and Shapiro-Wilk tests. PRS was not normally distributed (W = 0.808, p = 0.00348), while gait imbalance showed no significant departure from normality (W = 0.969, p = 0.8197). Therefore, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between the two datasets.

The analysis showed a weak negative correlation between PRS and gait imbalance (p = -0.232, p = 0.3852). However, this relationship was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

These results suggests that there is insufficient evidence of an association between polygenic risk and gait imbalance in the clinical trial patients. However, the small sample size (n = 16) limits the statistical power of this analysis. Other factors such as treatment, exercise and age, may also influence gait performance.

However the pattern is very different when patients are grouped by treatment. First I construct linear regression model within each group, then extract and run Shapiro test on residuals of each model to confirm normal distribution of residuals (both p>0.05). From the linear model (constructed using lm() and visualized on the plot), association between PRS and balance in patient group receiving L-DOPA is not statistically significant (linear regression p = 0.08), but there is a strong negative association between PRS and balance in the new treatment group (slope = -2.8, p = 0.008). This indicates PRS score is a potential predictor of response to the new compound, which is interesting because Parkinson disease patients tend to have higher PRS score than healthy people.
